Diabesity

Diabetes and Obesity (Diabesity): Weight Management, Insulin Resistance and Metabolic Health

Written by Dr. Ajay V. Kaduskar, Diabetologist, Nagpur

Dr. Ajay V. Kaduskar

MD (Medicine), PGDHSc (Diabetology), FEACD (The Netherlands)

Published 11 October 2026 · 15 minutes

Quick answer

Diabesity refers to type 2 diabetes occurring together with overweight or obesity. Fat stored deep in the abdomen (visceral fat) drives insulin resistance, which makes blood glucose harder to control. Treatment usually combines changes in eating, regular activity, attention to sleep, and medicines that help both blood sugar and weight. Losing even around 5% of body weight can improve blood sugar in many people.

When type 2 diabetes and obesity occur in the same person, doctors sometimes call it diabesity. The two are tied together mainly through insulin resistance: extra body fat, particularly fat stored around the abdomen, makes the body less responsive to insulin, and blood sugar gradually climbs. That's why weight management is usually part of diabetes treatment itself.

A lot of people hear "you need to lose weight" at the same appointment where they're told their sugar is high. It can feel like a lecture. It shouldn't be. This article explains what's going on in the body, why weight loss is often harder for people with diabetes, what treatment options exist today, and what kind of progress is realistic.

What is diabesity?

Diabesity isn't a separate diagnosis. It's shorthand for a very common combination: type 2 diabetes plus excess body weight.

The term exists because the two conditions keep pushing on each other. Carrying extra fat raises the chance of developing diabetes. Once diabetes is present, some of its effects, and some of the medicines used to treat it, can make weight harder to shift. If only the sugar readings get attention, results are often disappointing.

Is obesity a disease?

Yes. Most major medical bodies now classify obesity as a chronic medical condition. Body weight is shaped by genes, hormones, appetite signalling, sleep, stress, medicines, working hours, cost of food and plenty more. Nobody decides to develop obesity, and treating it with the same seriousness as high blood pressure tends to lead to better care and less shame.

How are diabetes and obesity connected?

Start with insulin. It's a hormone made by the pancreas, and after a meal it helps glucose move out of the blood and into muscle, liver and fat cells.

Excess fat, especially fat packed inside the abdomen and around the liver, interferes with this. Cells respond less well to insulin, so the pancreas makes more to get the same job done. For a few years that extra effort can keep blood sugar close to normal, and nothing seems wrong. Eventually the insulin-producing cells can't keep pace. Glucose starts to rise, first into the prediabetes range, then into diabetes.

The traffic runs both ways. High insulin levels encourage the body to store fat. Insulin and sulfonylureas can cause weight gain in some people. And fatigue, joint pain or low mood make it harder to stay active.

So when someone with diabetes says they're trying hard and the scale isn't moving, they're usually telling the truth. There's biology working against them.

What is insulin resistance?

Insulin resistance means the body's cells don't respond to insulin as well as they should, so glucose has trouble getting from the blood into the cells. The pancreas compensates by producing more insulin, which can mask the problem for a long time.

On its own, insulin resistance usually causes no clear symptoms. Some people notice their waist getting bigger, or feel heavy and sleepy after meals. Others develop dark, velvety skin at the back of the neck or in the armpits (acanthosis nigricans), or skin tags. None of these proves anything by itself. Fasting glucose, HbA1c, a lipid profile, blood pressure and a waist measurement together tell a doctor much more.

Why does belly fat matter more than body weight?

Fat under the skin, on the hips and thighs for example, is called subcutaneous fat. Fat stored deep inside the abdomen, wrapped around the liver, pancreas and intestines, is visceral fat.

Visceral fat is the more troublesome of the two. It's metabolically active and closely linked with insulin resistance, fatty liver, high triglycerides, raised blood pressure and heart disease. Two people who weigh exactly the same can have quite different risk depending on where that weight sits, which is why your doctor may reach for a measuring tape as well as the weighing scale.

Why are Indians at higher risk at a lower weight?

Studies have repeatedly found that many South Asians, Indians included, carry more abdominal and visceral fat and less muscle than some other populations at the same body weight. You'll sometimes hear this described as the "thin-fat" body type.

In practice, it means type 2 diabetes often shows up earlier and at a lower weight in Indians. Someone who looks only slightly overweight may already have marked insulin resistance. Indian guidelines use lower BMI and waist cut-offs for exactly this reason.

How is obesity measured in diabetes?

Doctors look at a few numbers together rather than relying on one.

MeasureWhat it tells youCut-offs commonly used for Indian adults
Body Mass Index (BMI)Weight in relation to heightNormal 18.5 to 22.9; overweight 23 to 24.9; obesity 25 and above
Waist circumferenceFat around the abdomenHigher risk at 90 cm or more (men), 80 cm or more (women)
Waist-to-height ratioAbdominal fat relative to heightA waist more than half your height suggests higher risk

BMI is a screening number. It can't tell fat from muscle and says nothing about where fat is stored. Where available, body composition analysis adds detail. Your doctor will read all of this alongside your blood reports, blood pressure and medical history.

What are the signs that diabetes and excess weight are affecting your health?

Early on, many people feel perfectly fine. That's part of the problem.

When symptoms do appear, the usual ones are thirst, passing urine more often, tiredness, blurred vision, cuts that take longer to heal, and repeated infections. Excess weight adds its own set: snoring, broken sleep, daytime sleepiness, getting breathless on stairs, and aching knees or back. Some people just notice their waist creeping up even though they haven't changed what they eat.

Because so much of this develops quietly, regular blood tests are more reliable than waiting to feel unwell.

Who is at higher risk of diabesity?

Your risk is higher if diabetes runs in your family, or if you've already been told you have prediabetes. A history of gestational diabetes or PCOS raises it too. So do high blood pressure, abnormal cholesterol, fatty liver and a waist above the cut-offs mentioned earlier. A desk-bound job, short or poor sleep, sleep apnoea and long-term use of certain medicines (steroids and some psychiatric drugs, for instance) all play a part.

Having one or more of these doesn't mean diabetes is inevitable. It does mean testing earlier and more regularly makes sense.

How is diabesity diagnosed and assessed?

Type 2 diabetes is confirmed with blood tests. Assessing diabesity goes a step further, looking at what the combination of high sugar and extra weight is doing elsewhere in the body.

What tests are commonly used?

  • Fasting plasma glucose: blood sugar after an overnight fast

  • HbA1c: a reflection of average blood sugar over roughly the last two to three months

  • Post-meal glucose or an oral glucose tolerance test: how well the body copes with a glucose load

  • Lipid profile: cholesterol and triglycerides

  • Liver function tests, and an ultrasound if fatty liver is suspected

  • Kidney function and urine albumin, to pick up early kidney changes

  • Blood pressure

  • Thyroid tests, if symptoms or history point that way

  • A sleep assessment, if there's heavy snoring, pauses in breathing or daytime sleepiness

Why look for underlying causes of weight gain?

Weight gain sometimes has a contributing medical cause: a hormonal problem, a sleep disorder, a medicine side effect. Finding it can change the whole plan. At Niramay Clinics, obesity care begins by looking for these possible causes before deciding on treatment.

How much weight loss improves Type 2 diabetes?

Less than most people expect, at least to start seeing benefits.

Diabetes guidelines generally say that losing about 5% of body weight can improve blood glucose, blood pressure and cholesterol in many people. Bigger losses that are kept off, often in the 10 to 15% range or beyond, usually bring larger improvements and sometimes allow medicines to be reduced.

If you weigh 90 kg, 5% is roughly 4.5 kg. That's a goal you can actually picture.

Can type 2 diabetes go into remission with weight loss?

For some people, yes, particularly if diabetes was diagnosed fairly recently. After substantial weight loss, blood sugar can return to the non-diabetic range. When HbA1c stays under 6.5% for at least three months without diabetes medicines, doctors call this remission.

Remission isn't a cure. Sugar can rise again, especially if weight comes back, so checks continue. And it isn't possible for everyone. People who don't reach remission still benefit a great deal from losing weight.

What are the treatment options for diabesity?

The plan should deal with blood sugar and weight at the same time, and it will change as you go.

Nutrition

No single diet suits everybody. The one that works is the one that brings your sugar down and that you can keep following on an ordinary Tuesday.

For most Indian households, a few things help. Try filling about half the plate with vegetables or salad, a quarter with protein such as dal, chana, rajma, paneer, curd, eggs, fish or chicken, and the remaining quarter with roti, rice or millets. Go easy on white rice, maida, biscuits, namkeen and sweet drinks, since these push sugar up quickly. Fruit juice counts as a sweet drink; whole fruit is a better choice. Having some protein at each meal helps with hunger and protects muscle while you lose weight. Fibre from whole grains, pulses and vegetables slows down how fast glucose enters the blood.

Festivals, weddings and restaurant meals are part of life. Planning a smaller portion usually works better than trying to avoid them altogether.

One caution. If you take insulin, or tablets that can cause low sugar, don't make big changes to your meals without talking to your doctor first.

Physical activity

Moving your muscles helps them take up glucose, and this happens even before any weight comes off.

For many adults with type 2 diabetes, the usual guidance is at least 150 minutes a week of moderate activity like brisk walking, spread over most days, plus strength exercise two or three times a week. That can be as simple as bodyweight squats, resistance bands or light weights. Breaking up long sitting spells with a few minutes of walking helps. So does a short walk after meals, which can blunt the post-meal sugar spike.

Start where you are and build slowly. If you have heart disease, foot problems or eye complications, or you're on insulin or sulfonylureas, ask your doctor what's safe for you and how to avoid low sugar during exercise.

Sleep and stress

Sleep gets overlooked. Short or poor sleep raises appetite, disturbs hunger hormones and worsens insulin resistance. Most adults do better with seven hours or more.

Loud snoring, stopping breathing at night or feeling drowsy through the day can point to obstructive sleep apnoea. It's common with obesity and makes both blood pressure and blood sugar harder to control, so mention it at your next visit.

Ongoing stress affects what and when people eat, and it can nudge blood sugar up. Dealing with it is a genuine part of treatment.

Medicines

Which medicine suits you depends on your sugar levels, weight, heart and kidney health, other conditions, cost and what you prefer. Diabetes medicines don't all affect weight in the same way.

GLP-1 and newer weight-management medicines

GLP-1 receptor agonists such as semaglutide, and tirzepatide, which acts on both GIP and GLP-1 pathways, have changed how doctors approach diabesity. They copy the action of gut hormones that regulate blood sugar, slow down stomach emptying and reduce appetite.

In many people they produce weight loss and sugar control that lifestyle changes alone couldn't achieve. They also come with trade-offs. Nausea, vomiting, constipation and loose motions are common in the first weeks. They aren't suitable for people with certain personal or family medical histories. Weight tends to come back if they're stopped without a plan for what comes next. They work better alongside good nutrition and strength exercise, and the cost adds up over months.

These are prescription medicines. They need a doctor's assessment and follow-up, and buying them from unreliable sources is risky.

Insulin

Many people with diabesity need insulin at some point to keep sugar safely under control. It isn't a punishment, and it doesn't mean diabetes has reached some final stage. If weight gain on insulin worries you, say so. Your doctor can often rework the plan, for example by pairing insulin with a medicine that supports weight loss. Don't cut down or stop insulin on your own.

Metabolic and bariatric surgery

For some people with severe obesity and type 2 diabetes, especially when other treatments haven't worked well enough, surgery may be an option. It can lead to large, lasting weight loss and big improvements in blood sugar. It's a serious step that needs careful evaluation beforehand and follow-up for life afterwards.

Monitoring and follow-up

Diabesity is long-term, so review visits are part of the treatment. Typically that means HbA1c every three to six months depending on how well controlled things are, along with weight, waist and blood pressure at each visit. Cholesterol and triglycerides get checked periodically. Kidney function and urine albumin are checked at least once a year, as are the eyes and feet. Liver tests are added if fatty liver is a concern.

Catching a drift early is much easier than dealing with a complication later.

Why is it so hard to lose weight with Type 2 diabetes?

This is probably the most common frustration we hear. "I've cut down on food and I'm still not losing weight."

Several things are usually going on at once. Insulin resistance and high insulin levels favour fat storage. Certain medicines raise appetite or add weight. When weight does come off, the body pushes back: hunger hormones rise and it burns a little less energy, which is why regain is so common. Low sugar episodes can lead to eating extra to correct them. Muscle declines with age and inactivity, lowering daily energy use. Then there's sleep, stress and long working hours, all of which affect appetite.

If you've been genuinely trying and nothing is changing, that's a reason to review the treatment. It isn't evidence that you've failed.

What happens if diabesity is not managed?

Years of high sugar, excess weight and the problems that travel with them raise the risk of complications. The main ones are heart attack and stroke, heart failure, kidney disease, damage to the retina (diabetic retinopathy), and nerve damage that causes numbness, tingling or pain in the feet. Fatty liver can progress to inflammation and scarring in some people. Sleep apnoea and knee arthritis are also more common.

None of these is guaranteed. Keeping sugar, blood pressure and cholesterol in range, not smoking and managing weight all lower the odds considerably.

How can complications be prevented?

It helps to think of prevention as several small levers pulled together rather than one big one.

Keep your blood sugar within the targets you've agreed with your doctor. Treat blood pressure and cholesterol, with medicines if needed. Aim for gradual weight loss and pay as much attention to keeping it off as to losing it. Stay active, and include strength work. Avoid tobacco in any form, including gutkha and kharra. Go for your yearly eye, kidney and foot checks even when you feel fine. And if a medicine is giving you side effects, tell your doctor rather than quietly stopping it.

When should you seek urgent medical help?

Go to a hospital or seek urgent care if there is:

  • Chest pain, pressure or tightness, particularly with breathlessness or sweating

  • Sudden weakness or numbness on one side, a drooping face or slurred speech

  • Confusion, unusual drowsiness or loss of consciousness

  • Low sugar symptoms (shaking, sweating, confusion) that don't settle after taking sugar, or a seizure

  • Repeated vomiting, especially with very high sugar readings or signs of dehydration

  • Severe abdominal pain that won't go away, particularly after starting a GLP-1 medicine

  • A foot wound that is red, swollen, warm, oozing or not healing

These can have more than one cause, and some are serious. They need to be seen quickly.

Common myths about diabetes and obesity

"It's just overeating." Food matters, but so do genes, hormones, sleep, medicines, stress and surroundings. Blame tends to delay care rather than improve it.

"There's no point unless I reach my ideal weight." Around 5% can already make a measurable difference.

"Starting insulin means it's all downhill." Insulin is safe and effective. Plenty of people use it for years while living full, active lives.

"Thin people don't get insulin resistance." Many Indians develop it, and diabetes too, at a normal-looking BMI because of abdominal fat.

"The injection will do the work." GLP-1 medicines do best with changes in diet and activity, and weight often returns when they're stopped without a plan.

"Skipping meals is the quickest way." On insulin or sulfonylureas, it's a quick way to a dangerous low. Supervised changes are much safer.

What to remember

  • Diabesity means type 2 diabetes and obesity together, linked mainly by insulin resistance.

  • Where fat sits matters. Abdominal fat carries more risk, and many Indians develop diabetes at lower weights.

  • About 5% weight loss can improve blood sugar; larger losses that are maintained help more and occasionally lead to remission.

  • Treatment brings together food, activity, sleep and medicines chosen for both sugar and weight, with a doctor guiding changes.

  • Regular checks of the heart, kidneys, eyes, liver and feet catch problems early.

Having diabetes and struggling with weight at the same time is tiring, and plenty of people have been told to "just lose weight" without any real help doing it. Treatment has moved on. There are more options now than there were even five years ago, and modest, steady progress counts for more than most people realise.

What works depends on your reports, your weight history, your other health conditions, your medicines and the life you actually live. A frank conversation with your doctor is a good place to start.

Frequently asked questions

Diabesity is a term for type 2 diabetes occurring together with overweight or obesity. It isn't a separate disease. It's used because the two conditions are so closely connected: fat stored around the abdomen increases insulin resistance, which pushes blood sugar up. Treating weight and sugar together generally works better than focusing on sugar readings alone.

Excess fat, especially visceral fat inside the abdomen, makes cells respond less well to insulin. The pancreas produces extra insulin to compensate, and for a while that keeps sugar near normal. Over time the pancreas can't keep up, and blood sugar rises into the prediabetes and then diabetes range. Family history, age and activity levels also affect who develops it.

Many people see better blood sugar, blood pressure and cholesterol after losing about 5% of their body weight. For someone weighing 80 kg, that's around 4 kg. Larger losses of 10 to 15% or more, kept off over time, usually bring bigger benefits and may allow some medicines to be reduced. Your doctor can help set a target that suits you.

In some people, particularly those diagnosed recently, significant weight loss brings blood sugar back to the non-diabetic range. Doctors call this remission when HbA1c stays below 6.5% for at least three months without diabetes medicines. It isn't a permanent cure. Sugar can rise again, especially with weight regain, so regular testing should continue.

Many Indians carry more fat around the abdomen and internal organs, and less muscle, than people of the same weight from some other populations. This raises insulin resistance even when BMI looks normal or only slightly high. Indian guidelines therefore use lower BMI and waist cut-offs to identify people at higher metabolic risk.

For suitable people, GLP-1 medicines can improve both blood sugar and weight. They're prescription medicines, though, and need a doctor's assessment first. Nausea, vomiting and constipation are common early side effects, and they aren't appropriate for people with certain medical histories. They work best alongside dietary changes and regular exercise, with ongoing medical follow-up.

Insulin can lead to some weight gain in certain people, partly because the body stops losing glucose in urine once sugar is better controlled. That isn't a reason to avoid or stop it. Doctors can often limit the effect through dose adjustments, meal planning, or combining insulin with medicines that help with weight.

For Indian adults, a waist of 90 cm or more in men and 80 cm or more in women is commonly used as a marker of higher metabolic risk. Waist size reflects abdominal fat, which is closely tied to insulin resistance. It's most useful read together with BMI, blood reports and blood pressure.
