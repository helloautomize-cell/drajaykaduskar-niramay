Technology and Evolution of GLP-1

The Technology & Evolution of GLP-1 Drugs: From Diabetes Injection to Global Weight-Loss Breakthrough

Written by Dr. Ajay V. Kaduskar, Diabetologist, Nagpur

Dr. Ajay V. Kaduskar

MD (Medicine), PGDHSc (Diabetology), FEACD (The Netherlands)

Published 9 October 2026 · 19 min

GLP-1 medicines seem like a very recent development.

The story behind GLP-1 drugs is much older than today's weight-loss trend

Over the last few years, names such as semaglutide and tirzepatide have become part of everyday conversations about diabetes, obesity and weight loss. People discuss them online, doctors are seeing more patients asking about them, and pharmaceutical companies are investing heavily in the next generation of these medicines.

But the science did not appear overnight.

The story began with a basic question about how the human body handles food.

Researchers were trying to understand why eating a meal produces a different insulin response from receiving the same amount of glucose directly into the bloodstream. That work eventually led to the discovery and study of hormones called incretins, including glucagon-like peptide-1, or GLP-1.

What followed was a long process of drug development.

Scientists had to figure out how GLP-1 worked, why the body's own GLP-1 disappeared so quickly, how to make a medicine that could imitate its effects, and eventually how to make that medicine last for days rather than minutes.

Today's once-weekly injections and newer oral medicines are the result of that progression.

In simple terms

GLP-1 drugs evolved from medicines designed primarily to control blood sugar in Type 2 diabetes into a broader group of metabolic treatments that can also influence appetite, body weight and other aspects of metabolic health. The technology has progressed from short-acting injections to longer-lasting weekly medicines, oral formulations and drugs designed to act on more than one hormonal pathway.

What exactly is GLP-1?

GLP-1 stands for glucagon-like peptide-1.

It is a hormone naturally produced by the intestine after we eat. One of its jobs is to help the body respond to food and rising blood glucose.

GLP-1 influences several processes at the same time. It can:

  • increase insulin secretion when blood glucose is elevated

  • reduce glucagon secretion

  • slow the movement of food from the stomach

  • influence feelings of fullness

  • affect appetite through pathways in the brain

That combination immediately made GLP-1 interesting to diabetes researchers.

There was, however, a major problem.

Natural GLP-1 does not stay in the body for very long.

It is rapidly broken down by enzymes, which means simply giving someone natural GLP-1 would not provide the sustained effect needed from a practical medicine.

So the challenge became one of drug engineering:

How can scientists make GLP-1-like treatment last long enough to be useful?

That question shaped the development of the entire drug class.

The early science: understanding the incretin effect

Before GLP-1 medicines existed, researchers had already noticed something unusual about insulin.

When glucose is consumed orally, the body can produce a stronger insulin response than when the same amount of glucose is given directly into the bloodstream.

Why?

Part of the answer was that the digestive system was not simply processing food. It was also communicating with the pancreas.

Hormones released after eating were helping the body prepare for the incoming nutrients.

These hormones became known as incretins.

GLP-1 emerged as one of the most important members of this system.

Research during the 1980s helped establish that active forms of GLP-1 could stimulate insulin secretion in response to glucose. Further work showed that GLP-1 could have useful effects in people with Type 2 diabetes.

The science was promising.

But natural GLP-1 still had one major weakness: it disappeared from the bloodstream almost immediately.

Drug developers needed another approach.

An unexpected clue came from the gila monster

One of the more unusual chapters in the development of GLP-1 medicines involves a desert-dwelling reptile.

Researchers studying the Gila monster identified a peptide called exendin-4 in its salivary secretions.

Exendin-4 had important similarities to GLP-1, but it was more resistant to rapid breakdown.

That made it scientifically interesting.

Instead of trying to use natural human GLP-1 directly, researchers now had a molecule that could activate the GLP-1 receptor and remain active considerably longer.

This work eventually contributed to the development of exenatide, one of the earliest GLP-1 receptor agonists used clinically.

Exenatide was approved in the United States in 2005 for the treatment of Type 2 diabetes.

This was an important milestone, but it was still only the beginning.

From short-acting injections to medicines that last much longer

The first GLP-1 medicines demonstrated that the basic idea worked.

The next challenge was convenience.

Early treatments required relatively frequent injections. For a chronic condition such as Type 2 diabetes, reducing how often a person has to administer a medicine can make a meaningful difference to everyday treatment.

Researchers therefore began modifying GLP-1-based molecules to extend their activity.

Over time, the field moved through several important stages:

StageDevelopment
Early GLP-1 therapyShorter-acting injectable medicines
Longer-acting moleculesLess frequent dosing
Daily treatmentMore sustained receptor activation
Weekly injectionsMuch less frequent administration
Oral formulationsGLP-1 treatment without an injection
Multi-receptor medicinesMore than one metabolic pathway targeted

Medicines such as liraglutide, dulaglutide and semaglutide illustrate different stages of this evolution.

The underlying principle remained similar, but the pharmacology became increasingly sophisticated.

Why once-weekly treatment was such a big development

Think about the difference between remembering a medication every day and taking it once a week.

That does not mean weekly treatment is automatically better for every patient. It does, however, make the treatment schedule considerably different from the early GLP-1 medicines.

Semaglutide became particularly important in this respect.

Its molecular design allowed it to remain active for much longer than natural GLP-1, making once-weekly injection possible.

Semaglutide was initially developed for Type 2 diabetes. Its effects on body weight subsequently became a major part of its clinical development, leading to its use in chronic weight management under a separate indication.

This was one of the moments when public understanding of GLP-1 medicines began to change.

They were no longer being discussed only in diabetes clinics.

Weight management had become a major part of the conversation.

Why do GLP-1 medicines cause weight loss?

The answer is more complicated than simply saying that they "burn fat."

They don't work like traditional fat-burning supplements.

Their effects are tied to several physiological processes.

GLP-1 receptor activation can increase feelings of fullness, influence appetite-related brain pathways and slow gastric emptying. As a result, some people eat less food without experiencing the same level of hunger they previously experienced.

Over time, reduced energy intake can lead to weight loss.

That is an important distinction.

GLP-1 medicines do not replace the body's need for energy balance. They influence some of the biological signals that regulate hunger, fullness and food intake.

This is one reason the medicines attracted so much interest in obesity treatment.

The shift from diabetes treatment to obesity medicine

The transition did not happen because scientists suddenly discovered that people lost weight while taking GLP-1 drugs.

Weight-related effects had been observed and studied along the way.

The bigger change was recognizing that these effects could be therapeutically important in their own right.

Liraglutide became one of the earlier GLP-1 medicines to receive an obesity indication.

Semaglutide later produced much greater public attention when a higher-dose formulation was approved for chronic weight management.

That changed the conversation around the entire drug class.

A treatment originally associated with blood sugar control was now being discussed as part of medical obesity treatment.

And that distinction matters.

Obesity is a chronic disease with complex biological, behavioural and environmental contributors. Treating it is not simply a matter of telling someone to "eat less."

The development of GLP-1 medicines highlighted how strongly biological appetite regulation can influence long-term weight management.

Then came tirzepatide, and the idea got bigger

If semaglutide represented an important development in GLP-1 therapy, tirzepatide represented a different idea.

Tirzepatide does not act only on the GLP-1 receptor.

It activates two incretin-related receptors: GIP and GLP-1.

That is why it is described as a dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist.

GIP stands for glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide.

Like GLP-1, GIP is involved in the body's response to nutrients.

Instead of targeting one hormonal pathway, researchers were now exploring whether combining two pathways could produce a broader metabolic effect.

Tirzepatide was approved for Type 2 diabetes and later for chronic weight management.

This opened the door to a bigger question:

If two metabolic signals can be combined, what happens when scientists deliberately design medicines around three?

That is where the next generation of research comes in.

What are dual and triple agonists?

The terminology can sound complicated, but the basic concept is fairly straightforward.

A single agonist activates one receptor.

A dual agonist activates two.

A triple agonist is designed to activate three.

Tirzepatide is an example of a dual agonist because it targets:

  • GIP

  • GLP-1

One investigational medicine attracting attention is retatrutide, which is designed to act on:

  • GIP

  • GLP-1

  • glucagon

This is an area of ongoing clinical research.

It is important not to blur the line between research and approved treatment. A promising result from a clinical trial does not mean that a medicine is already available for routine treatment.

The purpose of these studies is to find out whether the theoretical benefits of targeting several pathways translate into meaningful benefits while maintaining an acceptable safety profile.

The injection pen has evolved too

Drug development is not only about the molecule inside the medicine.

There is another part patients interact with directly: the delivery device.

Modern injection pens are very different from the traditional image many people have of medication injections.

Prefilled pens and other delivery systems are designed to simplify administration, provide controlled doses and reduce the number of steps involved.

That matters for long-term treatment.

A sophisticated medicine is of limited practical value if its delivery method is unnecessarily difficult for the person using it.

So the evolution of GLP-1 treatment has happened on two fronts:

The medicine

Researchers have worked on:

  • longer duration

  • more predictable exposure

  • receptor selectivity

  • multiple receptor activity

  • alternative chemical structures

The delivery system

Researchers and manufacturers have worked on:

  • prefilled devices

  • easier dose administration

  • fewer injections

  • simpler handling

The two developments have progressed alongside each other.

Can GLP-1 medicines be taken as tablets?

For years, an oral GLP-1 medicine seemed like a particularly difficult technical problem.

Why?

Because many GLP-1 drugs are peptides.

Peptides are not naturally well suited to being swallowed as conventional tablets. The digestive system can break them down before enough of the medicine reaches the bloodstream.

Scientists therefore had to solve two problems:

  1. protect the medicine from degradation

  2. help enough of it get absorbed

Oral semaglutide demonstrated that this could be done.

It became an important milestone because patients could receive a GLP-1 receptor agonist without using an injection.

But the story does not end there.

The move from peptide drugs to small molecules

One of the most interesting developments in the field is the attempt to create small-molecule GLP-1 receptor agonists.

This is technically different from simply putting an injectable peptide into a tablet.

A small molecule can have different absorption and formulation characteristics, potentially making oral administration more straightforward.

Orforglipron is an example of this newer approach. It is a non-peptide, orally administered GLP-1 receptor agonist.

This is an important step in the evolution of the technology because it shows that researchers are not simply modifying the original class of injectable medicines.

They are exploring different molecular strategies to stimulate the same biological pathway.

Does this mean injections will disappear?

Probably not in the simple sense of "tablets replace injections."

Different medicines have different properties.

An oral medicine and an injectable medicine may differ in:

  • pharmacokinetics

  • dosing

  • absorption

  • tolerability

  • effectiveness

  • approved indications

  • patient suitability

Some people may strongly prefer a tablet.

Others may find a weekly injection easier than taking a medicine every day under specific administration requirements.

The important development is that patients may eventually have more options, rather than only one way of accessing GLP-1-based treatment.

Why is the pharmaceutical industry looking beyond GLP-1 alone?

Human metabolism is not controlled by a single hormone.

Appetite, insulin secretion, glucagon activity, energy expenditure and nutrient storage are connected through several biological systems.

That is why multi-receptor medicines have become such an important research area.

The goal is not simply to make a "stronger GLP-1."

Researchers are trying to understand whether carefully combining hormonal signals can produce a more useful metabolic response.

That could potentially influence several outcomes at the same time.

But this is precisely where scientific enthusiasm needs to be balanced with evidence.

A drug that looks promising in early studies still needs larger and longer clinical trials.

Safety matters just as much as efficacy.

What could the next generation of GLP-1 medicines look like?

Nobody can say exactly what the final form of future treatment will be.

Several directions, however, are already visible.

1. more oral medicines

The success of oral GLP-1 formulations has shown that injection-free treatment is possible.

Future research may produce additional oral medicines with different dosing schedules and pharmacological characteristics.

2. multi-hormone medicines

Dual and triple agonists are being investigated because metabolism involves several interconnected pathways.

3. longer treatment intervals

Researchers continue to look for ways of maintaining therapeutic effects while reducing how frequently medication needs to be administered.

4. better delivery systems

Injection devices are likely to continue becoming easier to use.

5. more personalized treatment

As the field develops, clinicians may have a wider range of medicines to choose from rather than treating every patient with the same approach.

The question may increasingly become:

Which metabolic pathway and treatment format best fits this particular patient?

Why did GLP-1 medicines suddenly become such a global phenomenon?

The science took decades.

The public conversation did not.

Several things happened almost at the same time.

The newer medicines produced meaningful improvements in blood glucose and body weight. Weekly injections made long-term treatment more practical. Obesity became increasingly recognized as a chronic medical condition rather than simply a lifestyle issue.

Then social media accelerated everything.

People began sharing personal experiences, weight-loss stories and opinions about GLP-1 medicines. The discussion moved beyond medical journals and diabetes clinics into mainstream culture.

That created enormous interest.

But popularity also creates problems.

Online conversations often remove important context.

A medicine prescribed for a specific patient can become simplified into a social-media phrase such as "weight-loss injection." That description leaves out the pharmacology, indications, contraindications, side effects, monitoring and long-term treatment considerations.

For patients, understanding the science is more useful than following the trend.

The most important point: GLP-1 drugs are still medicines

The rapid development of this technology can make GLP-1 drugs seem almost futuristic.

They are not.

They are prescription medicines that affect real physiological systems.

That means they can also cause adverse effects and may not be appropriate for everyone.

The suitability of a GLP-1-based medicine can depend on factors such as:

  • diabetes status

  • obesity or overweight status

  • other medical conditions

  • current medicines

  • gastrointestinal symptoms

  • previous treatment history

  • treatment goals

  • pregnancy status where relevant

  • specific contraindications associated with the medicine

A medicine being newer, more expensive or more widely discussed does not automatically make it the right choice.

What the evolution of GLP-1 drugs really tells us

The most interesting part of this story isn't simply that GLP-1 medicines can help people lose weight.

It is how the technology got there.

Researchers began with a naturally occurring hormone that disappeared from the bloodstream within minutes.

They then found ways to reproduce its effects for longer.

The first medicines were injectable.

Then came longer-acting versions.

Daily treatment became weekly treatment.

GLP-1 therapy expanded from diabetes into obesity medicine.

Then researchers combined GLP-1 with another hormonal pathway.

Oral formulations followed.

Now researchers are studying small molecules and medicines that act on three metabolic pathways.

In other words, the field has moved from replacing a short-lived hormone signal to deliberately engineering complex metabolic signals.

That is why the history of GLP-1 drugs is more than a story about weight loss.

It is a story about how modern drug development can take an observation from basic biology and gradually turn it into an entirely new treatment platform.

What patients should take away from this evolution

If you are hearing more about GLP-1 medicines today, it helps to remember that there is a lot of science underneath the headlines.

The medicines available today are not identical.

Some primarily target GLP-1.

Some target GLP-1 and GIP.

Some are injectable.

Some are oral.

Some newer multi-hormone medicines are still being investigated.

So the useful question is not simply:

"Which GLP-1 drug is the newest?"

A better starting point is:

"Is this type of treatment appropriate for my health condition, and what would treatment involve in my case?"

That is a clinical question.

It requires an assessment rather than an internet comparison.

When should you speak to a doctor about GLP-1 treatment?

Consider discussing GLP-1 treatment with a qualified doctor if you have:

  • Type 2 diabetes that needs better control

  • obesity or excess weight associated with health problems

  • difficulty achieving sustainable weight loss despite appropriate lifestyle measures

  • questions about whether a GLP-1 or related medicine is appropriate for you

  • concerns about side effects from an existing metabolic medicine

  • questions about switching between diabetes or weight-management treatments

Do not start, stop or change a prescription GLP-1 medicine solely because of advice from social media or an online article.

The GLP-1 story is a good example of how medical innovation usually happens.

There was no single moment when scientists "invented" the modern weight-loss drug.

The progress came in stages.

First came an understanding of incretin biology. Then researchers found ways around the extremely short lifespan of natural GLP-1. Early injections proved the concept. Longer-acting medicines improved the treatment experience. Semaglutide helped establish the importance of GLP-1 therapy in weight management. Tirzepatide expanded the approach to another hormonal pathway. Oral formulations tackled the challenge of non-injectable treatment.

Now the research is moving toward multi-hormone medicines and entirely different molecular designs.

Where this ultimately leads is still being worked out.

For patients, the most useful thing is not to chase every new drug that appears in the news. It is to understand what these medicines actually do, what their evidence shows, and whether a particular treatment makes sense for their own health.

GLP-1 technology has changed dramatically. The clinical principle has not: the right treatment is the one that is appropriate for the individual patient, not simply the newest treatment on the market.

When to seek urgent care

Seek urgent care for severe or persistent vomiting, severe upper stomach pain spreading to the back, fainting, or symptoms of very low sugar while on these medicines.
