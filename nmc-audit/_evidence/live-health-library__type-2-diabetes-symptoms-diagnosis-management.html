Type 2 Diabetes

Type 2 Diabetes: Symptoms, Diagnosis, Blood Sugar Control & Long-Term Management

Written by Dr. Ajay V. Kaduskar, Diabetologist, Nagpur

Dr. Ajay V. Kaduskar

MD (Medicine), PGDHSc (Diabetology), FEACD (The Netherlands)

Published 1 October 2026 · 15 min

Type 2 diabetes rarely begins with one dramatic symptom. For many people, blood sugar rises gradually, sometimes for years, without causing anything that feels obviously wrong. Others notice increased thirst, frequent urination, unusual tiredness or changes in their weight and only then decide to get tested.

That is one reason diabetes can be easy to overlook in its early stages.

Type 2 diabetes develops when the body becomes less responsive to insulin and, over time, the pancreas may not be able to produce enough insulin to keep blood glucose within a healthy range. The condition is common, but the way it develops and the treatment a person needs can vary considerably.

Managing it is not simply a matter of avoiding sugar.

Food choices, physical activity, body weight, sleep, medications, blood pressure, cholesterol, kidney health and cardiovascular risk can all form part of diabetes care. For some people, lifestyle changes make a substantial difference. Others need one or more medicines, and some eventually require insulin.

The aim is not to chase a single “perfect” blood sugar reading. Good diabetes care means keeping glucose in an appropriate range while also reducing the risk of problems involving the heart, kidneys, eyes, nerves and feet.

Type 2 diabetes in simple terms

Type 2 diabetes is a long-term metabolic condition in which blood glucose becomes persistently elevated.

The main problem usually involves insulin resistance. Insulin is a hormone that helps glucose move from the bloodstream into cells, where it can be used for energy. When the body's cells respond poorly to insulin, the pancreas has to work harder to maintain normal glucose levels.

For a period of time, the pancreas may compensate by producing more insulin. Eventually, however, insulin production may no longer be enough to overcome the body's insulin resistance.

Blood glucose then begins to rise.

This process is not identical in everyone. Genetics, excess body fat, physical inactivity, age, family history and other metabolic factors can contribute to the development of Type 2 diabetes.

What are the symptoms of Type 2 diabetes?

Some people have clear symptoms. Others have none.

Common symptoms include:

  • Passing urine more frequently than usual

  • Increased thirst

  • Feeling unusually tired

  • Increased hunger

  • Unexplained weight loss in some cases

  • Blurred vision

  • Slow-healing cuts or wounds

  • Recurrent infections

  • Tingling or numbness in the hands or feet

The difficulty is that several of these symptoms are nonspecific. Tiredness, for example, can have many causes.

That is why symptoms alone cannot establish whether someone has diabetes. A blood test is needed for diagnosis.

Can you have Type 2 diabetes without symptoms?

Yes.

This is one of the reasons routine testing matters, particularly for people who have risk factors for diabetes.

Someone can have elevated blood glucose and feel completely normal. By the time symptoms become noticeable, blood sugar may already have been elevated for some time.

If you have a strong family history of diabetes, excess weight, high blood pressure, abnormal cholesterol or other risk factors, speak with a doctor about whether you should be tested.

How is Type 2 diabetes diagnosed?

Diabetes is diagnosed using specific blood glucose or HbA1c criteria.

The commonly used diagnostic thresholds include:

TestDiabetes range
HbA1c6.5% or higher
Fasting plasma glucose126 mg/dL or higher
2-hour glucose during a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test200 mg/dL or higher
Random plasma glucose with classic symptoms or hyperglycaemic crisis200 mg/dL or higher

An abnormal result does not always mean that the diagnosis can be made immediately, particularly when a person has no obvious symptoms. In many situations, the result needs to be confirmed with repeat testing or another diagnostic test.

The exact test used can depend on the person's circumstances.

What does HbA1c mean?

HbA1c is one of the most useful tests in diabetes care.

Unlike a glucose test that tells you the blood sugar level at one particular point in time, HbA1c provides an indication of average blood glucose exposure over roughly the previous three months.

It does not require fasting.

For diagnosis, an HbA1c of 6.5% or above falls within the diabetes range. An HbA1c between 5.7% and 6.4% is generally classified as prediabetes.

But HbA1c should not be interpreted in isolation.

Certain conditions affecting red blood cells, haemoglobin, blood loss or other medical factors can affect how reliable the result is. If the HbA1c does not appear to fit the clinical picture, your doctor may recommend another form of testing.

What HbA1c should a person with diabetes aim for?

There is no single HbA1c target that is appropriate for every person.

For many adults with diabetes, a target around 7% or lower may be appropriate, but the goal should be individualized. Age, duration of diabetes, other medical conditions, medications, risk of hypoglycaemia and the person's overall treatment situation all matter.

Lower is not automatically better if achieving a very low glucose level creates significant risk.

The target should therefore be discussed with the treating doctor rather than copied from someone else's diabetes plan.

Fasting blood sugar and post-meal blood sugar: what's the difference?

Both measurements can provide useful information, but they answer slightly different questions.

Fasting blood glucose is measured after a period without food, usually after at least eight hours of fasting when used for diagnostic testing.

Post-meal glucose looks at how blood sugar behaves after eating. Depending on when it is measured, it can help identify glucose rises that may not be obvious from a fasting reading alone.

A person may have a reasonable fasting glucose but still experience substantial rises after meals.

That is why diabetes management sometimes involves looking at patterns rather than focusing on one number.

What causes Type 2 diabetes?

There is rarely one single cause.

Several factors can contribute, including:

  • Family history of Type 2 diabetes

  • Insulin resistance

  • Excess body weight or abdominal fat

  • Physical inactivity

  • Increasing age

  • Previous gestational diabetes

  • High blood pressure

  • Abnormal cholesterol or triglycerides

  • Certain medications or medical conditions

Having one or more risk factors does not mean that diabetes is inevitable.

Likewise, someone without an obvious risk factor can still develop the condition.

How is Type 2 diabetes treated?

Treatment is usually built around several parts rather than one intervention.

Depending on the individual, management may include:

  1. Nutrition and portion control

  2. Regular physical activity

  3. Weight management where appropriate

  4. Diabetes medicines

  5. Insulin when indicated

  6. Blood glucose monitoring

  7. Blood pressure and cholesterol management

  8. Screening for diabetes-related complications

  9. Regular review of the treatment plan

The plan can change over time.

A person who needs medication today may require a different combination later. Conversely, someone whose diabetes is initially well controlled through lifestyle changes may eventually need medication as the disease progresses.

That does not mean treatment has “failed.” Type 2 diabetes is a chronic condition, and its treatment often needs to evolve.

What should you eat if you have Type 2 diabetes?

A diabetes-friendly diet does not have to mean giving up every food you enjoy.

For many Indian households, the practical questions are much more specific:

Can I eat roti?

What about rice?

Are fruits allowed?

What should I have for breakfast?

Can I eat sweets occasionally?

The answer usually comes down to portion size, carbohydrate quantity and quality, overall diet, meal pattern and individual treatment needs.

A useful starting point is to build meals around vegetables, appropriate protein sources, high-fibre foods and controlled portions of carbohydrate-containing foods.

Foods such as whole grains, pulses, vegetables, nuts and seeds can often fit into a balanced eating pattern.

Sugary drinks, frequent sweets and highly processed foods are generally less helpful for blood glucose management.

But there is no universal “diabetes food list” that works equally well for everyone.

Your diet should take into account your blood glucose pattern, weight goals, medications, activity level, food preferences and other health conditions.

Does exercise help control blood sugar?

Yes.

Physical activity can improve insulin sensitivity and help the body use glucose more effectively. It can also support weight management and cardiovascular health.

The right exercise plan depends on the person's age, fitness level, complications and other medical conditions.

For someone who has been inactive for a long time, starting with manageable activity and gradually increasing it may be more realistic than suddenly attempting intense workouts.

Walking, resistance training and other forms of aerobic activity can all have a role.

If you have diabetes-related complications or significant cardiovascular problems, discuss an exercise plan with your doctor before starting strenuous activity.

When are medicines needed?

Lifestyle changes are an important part of diabetes management, but they are not always sufficient on their own.

Depending on the person's glucose levels, cardiovascular and kidney health, body weight, risk of hypoglycaemia and other factors, a doctor may consider medicines such as:

  • Metformin

  • SGLT2 inhibitors

  • GLP-1 receptor agonists

  • GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists

  • DPP-4 inhibitors

  • Sulfonylureas

  • Thiazolidinediones

  • Insulin

These medicines work in different ways.

Some improve insulin sensitivity. Some increase glucose loss through the urine. Others influence insulin secretion, appetite or glucose regulation.

The choice is not simply about which medicine lowers blood sugar the most. Doctors also consider side effects, kidney function, cardiovascular disease, weight, cost, hypoglycaemia risk and other individual factors.

A medicine that is suitable for one patient may not be appropriate for another.

Does everyone with Type 2 diabetes need insulin?

No.

Some people with Type 2 diabetes can manage their blood glucose without insulin, particularly during earlier stages of the disease.

Others may need insulin temporarily or permanently.

Insulin can become necessary when the pancreas is no longer producing enough insulin, when glucose levels are markedly elevated, during certain illnesses or situations, or when other treatment approaches are insufficient.

Needing insulin is not a sign that someone has done something wrong.

It is simply one of the treatment options available when the body requires additional insulin support.

How often should blood sugar be checked?

There is no single monitoring schedule for every person with Type 2 diabetes.

Someone taking insulin may need to monitor more frequently than someone whose diabetes is controlled with lifestyle measures or certain non-insulin medicines.

Some patients may also benefit from continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), depending on their treatment and clinical circumstances.

HbA1c is another part of follow-up and is commonly checked at regular intervals, with the frequency determined by the person's treatment plan and level of glucose control.

The useful question is not simply, “How many times should I check my sugar?”

It is:

What information do we need from monitoring to make the next treatment decision?

What complications can Type 2 diabetes cause?

Persistently high blood glucose can affect blood vessels and nerves over time.

Important complications include:

Eye disease

Diabetes can damage the small blood vessels supplying the retina. Regular eye examinations can help detect changes before vision is seriously affected.

Kidney disease

Diabetes is an important cause of chronic kidney disease. Blood and urine tests can help assess kidney function and detect early changes.

Nerve damage

Diabetic neuropathy may cause burning, tingling, numbness or altered sensation, particularly in the feet.

Foot problems

Reduced sensation and poor circulation can make seemingly minor foot injuries more serious. Regular foot checks are therefore an important part of diabetes care.

Heart and blood vessel disease

Diabetes is associated with increased cardiovascular risk. Blood pressure, cholesterol, smoking status, weight and other risk factors therefore deserve attention alongside glucose.

The purpose of regular screening is not to create anxiety. It is to identify problems early enough to act on them.

What health checks should someone with Type 2 diabetes have?

A diabetes follow-up appointment may involve more than checking blood glucose.

Depending on the individual, regular care may include:

  • HbA1c

  • Blood pressure

  • Cholesterol and other lipid measurements

  • Kidney function

  • Urine albumin testing

  • Eye examination

  • Foot examination

  • Weight and, where appropriate, waist measurements

  • Medication review

  • Cardiovascular risk assessment

The exact schedule varies according to the person's age, diabetes duration, treatment and existing complications.

Can Type 2 diabetes be reversed?

This question needs careful wording.

Some people with Type 2 diabetes can achieve remission, particularly after substantial and sustained weight loss or other major metabolic changes.

Remission is not the same thing as saying that diabetes has been permanently cured.

Blood glucose can rise again, particularly if weight is regained or the underlying metabolic problems return.

For that reason, even someone whose glucose levels have returned to the non-diabetes range should continue appropriate follow-up.

Why is my blood sugar still high even though i take medicine?

This is a common situation and does not necessarily mean the medication is ineffective.

Blood glucose can be influenced by:

  • Food intake

  • Physical activity

  • Stress

  • Poor sleep

  • Illness or infection

  • Weight changes

  • Missed doses

  • Medication timing

  • Progressive changes in insulin production

  • Other medicines

  • Changes in insulin sensitivity

Rather than increasing or stopping medication independently, persistent high readings should be discussed with the treating doctor.

Sometimes the issue is the medication itself. Sometimes the treatment is appropriate but needs adjustment because the person's metabolic situation has changed.

When should you see a diabetologist?

Consider seeking specialist diabetes care if:

  • Your blood sugar remains above your agreed target

  • Your HbA1c continues to rise

  • You have recently been diagnosed and need a treatment plan

  • You are having repeated episodes of low blood sugar

  • You are considering insulin

  • You have diabetes with obesity or other metabolic conditions

  • You have kidney, eye, nerve, heart or foot-related concerns

  • Your current treatment is causing troublesome side effects

  • You are unsure how to interpret your glucose readings

Early specialist input can also be useful when several metabolic risk factors are present together.

When is high blood sugar an emergency?

Very high or very low glucose can sometimes become an emergency.

Seek urgent medical attention if diabetes is accompanied by symptoms such as:

  • Confusion or marked drowsiness

  • Loss of consciousness

  • Severe weakness

  • Persistent vomiting

  • Difficulty breathing

  • Severe dehydration

  • Symptoms suggesting a serious acute metabolic complication

People taking glucose-lowering medicines should also know the warning signs of hypoglycaemia, particularly sweating, shaking, hunger, dizziness, confusion or unusual behaviour.

Emergency symptoms should not be managed by adjusting medication at home without appropriate medical advice.

What does successful diabetes management actually look like?

It is easy to think of diabetes management as a daily battle with a glucose meter.

In practice, it is broader than that.

Good management means understanding your condition, knowing your treatment, monitoring when monitoring is useful, staying physically active, eating in a sustainable way and attending recommended follow-ups.

It also means looking beyond blood sugar.

Blood pressure, cholesterol, kidney health, eye health, weight, cardiovascular risk and foot health are all relevant.

There will also be days when the numbers are not perfect.

The goal is not perfection. The goal is sustained management that reduces the risk of complications while fitting realistically into everyday life.

The bottom line

Type 2 diabetes is manageable, but it needs more than occasional blood sugar checks.

The earlier the condition is identified and the more consistently it is followed, the more opportunities there are to address glucose levels and other cardiovascular and metabolic risk factors.

Treatment may involve food and exercise changes, medication, insulin, glucose monitoring or a combination of these. The right approach depends on the individual rather than a standard plan copied from someone else.

If your blood sugar or HbA1c is repeatedly elevated, or you have been diagnosed with Type 2 diabetes and are unsure what to do next, a consultation with a diabetes specialist can help clarify the diagnosis, treatment options and follow-up plan.
