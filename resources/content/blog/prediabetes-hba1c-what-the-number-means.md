**Quick answer.** HbA1c below 5.7 percent is generally considered normal, 5.7 to 6.4 percent indicates prediabetes, and 6.5 percent or higher on repeated testing suggests diabetes. A prediabetes result is a signal to act now with weight management, physical activity and healthier eating, plus regular follow-up testing.

HbA1c, also called glycated haemoglobin or A1C, is a blood test that shows your average blood sugar over the past two to three months. It is one of the main tests used to detect prediabetes and diabetes. Understanding your HbA1c helps you act early to prevent type 2 diabetes.

#### What Does HbA1c Measure?

Glucose in the blood attaches to haemoglobin in red blood cells. Because red cells live about three months, the percentage of haemoglobin with glucose attached reflects average sugar levels over that time. Unlike a single sugar reading, HbA1c is not affected by what you ate that morning.

#### How Do You Read Your Result?

- Below 5.7 percent: normal range

- 5.7 to 6.4 percent: prediabetes

- 6.5 percent or above: diabetes, usually confirmed with a repeat test or another test

Within the prediabetes range, a higher number such as 6.2 to 6.4 percent suggests a greater risk of progressing to diabetes, so prompt action is especially important.

#### What Can Affect HbA1c Accuracy?

HbA1c may be less reliable in people with:

- Anaemia or iron deficiency

- Haemoglobin variants such as thalassaemia trait or sickle cell trait

- Recent blood loss or transfusion

- Pregnancy

- Advanced kidney or liver disease

In these situations, doctors may use fasting glucose or a glucose tolerance test instead.

#### What Should You Do If HbA1c Shows Prediabetes?

##### Lose excess weight

Losing about 5 to 7 percent of body weight can lower the risk of progressing to diabetes.

##### Be active

Aim for about 150 minutes of moderate activity each week, such as brisk walking, plus strength training.

##### Improve eating habits

Reduce refined carbohydrates, sugary drinks and sweets. Add vegetables, pulses, protein and whole grains.

##### Sleep and stress

Good sleep and stress management support better sugar control.

##### Medicines

In some high-risk people, doctors may consider metformin.

#### How Often Should You Retest?

People with prediabetes are usually retested at least once a year, and sometimes every three to six months while making changes. Your doctor will guide timing.

#### Key Takeaways

- HbA1c shows average sugar over about three months.

- 5.7 to 6.4 percent indicates prediabetes.

- Some conditions can affect accuracy.

- Weight loss, activity and diet changes lower risk.

- Retest regularly to track progress.

#### Final Thoughts

A prediabetes HbA1c is a valuable early warning, not a diagnosis of diabetes. Acting now can bring your numbers back toward normal. Dr. Ajay V. Kaduskar at Niramay Clinics in Nagpur helps patients interpret results and plan prevention.

#### When to seek urgent care

Seek urgent care for extreme thirst with vomiting, confusion, deep rapid breathing or unusual drowsiness, which can signal very high sugar.

#### Frequently asked questions

**What HbA1c level is prediabetes?**

An HbA1c between 5.7 and 6.4 percent is generally considered prediabetes. Below 5.7 percent is normal, and 6.5 percent or higher suggests diabetes when confirmed.

**Is HbA1c 6.0 serious?**

An HbA1c of 6.0 percent is in the prediabetes range and indicates higher risk of developing type 2 diabetes. It is a strong signal to start lifestyle changes and regular monitoring.

**Can HbA1c be wrong?**

HbA1c can be less accurate in anaemia, haemoglobin variants, pregnancy, recent blood loss or kidney disease. Your doctor may use other tests in these situations.

**How quickly can HbA1c improve?**

Because HbA1c reflects about three months of sugar levels, changes usually show after around three months of consistent lifestyle changes. Some improvement may be visible sooner.

**Do I need medicine for prediabetes?**

Most people start with lifestyle changes. Doctors may consider metformin for some high-risk individuals. Discuss your situation with your doctor.
