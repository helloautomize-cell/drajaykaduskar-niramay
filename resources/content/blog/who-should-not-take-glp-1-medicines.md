**Quick answer.** GLP-1 medicines should not be used by people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid cancer or MEN2 syndrome, by those who have had a serious allergic reaction to them, and during pregnancy or breastfeeding. They need special caution in people with a history of pancreatitis, gallbladder disease, severe stomach or bowel problems, kidney disease, diabetic eye disease and type 1 diabetes. Always get a medical assessment first.

GLP-1 medicines help many people with diabetes and obesity. But they are not right for everyone. Some people should avoid them completely, and others need extra care. This guide explains who and why, so you can have an informed talk with your doctor.

#### Medullary thyroid cancer or mEN2

These medicines carry a warning about thyroid C-cell tumours seen in animal studies. They are not advised for people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or the genetic condition MEN2.

#### Serious allergy

Anyone who has had a severe allergic reaction to a GLP-1 medicine or its ingredients should not take it.

#### Pregnancy and breastfeeding

They are not recommended during pregnancy or while breastfeeding. Women planning pregnancy are usually advised to stop the medicine well before trying to conceive. Ask your doctor how long.

#### Who needs extra caution?

#### History of pancreatitis

GLP-1 medicines have been linked to pancreatitis. People who have had it need specialist advice, and many doctors avoid these medicines.

#### Gallstones or gallbladder disease

Rapid weight loss and these medicines can raise the risk of gallstones and gallbladder inflammation.

#### Severe stomach or bowel disease

Gastroparesis (very slow stomach emptying), severe reflux or bowel obstruction can worsen with slowed digestion.

#### Kidney disease

Vomiting and dehydration can strain the kidneys. Dose adjustments or close monitoring may be needed.

#### Diabetic retinopathy

In people with advanced eye disease, rapid improvement in blood sugar has been linked with temporary worsening of retinopathy in some studies. An eye check is advised before and during treatment.

#### Type 1 diabetes

These medicines are not standard treatment for type 1 diabetes, and they do not replace insulin.

#### Eating disorders

People with a history of anorexia, bulimia or binge eating need careful assessment. Appetite-reducing medicines can worsen such conditions.

#### Low body weight or frailty

Older adults who are frail or underweight may lose muscle and become weaker.

#### Other medicines

People on insulin or sulfonylureas have a higher risk of low blood sugar, and doses may need adjustment. Some oral medicines may be absorbed differently as stomach emptying slows.

#### Planned surgery

Because of slowed stomach emptying, anaesthetists may ask you to pause the medicine before surgery or sedation. Tell your surgeon and anaesthetist.

#### Is weight loss without obesity safe?

These medicines are meant for people with diabetes, obesity (or overweight with weight-related disease). Using them for small cosmetic weight loss is not recommended, and risks may outweigh benefits.

#### What checks should you expect before starting?

- Your medical and family history

- A list of all your medicines

- Weight, BMI and waist

- Blood tests for HbA1c, kidney, liver and sometimes lipid levels

- Eye examination if you have diabetes

- Pregnancy plans

- A discussion about expectations, cost and follow-up

#### When should you see a doctor?

Always consult a diabetologist before starting. Contact your doctor at once if, during treatment, you have severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, a neck lump, hoarseness, trouble swallowing, vision changes or signs of an allergy.

#### The quick version

- Avoid GLP-1 medicines if you have a personal or family history of medullary thyroid cancer or MEN2, or a serious allergy.

- Do not use them in pregnancy or breastfeeding.

- Take extra caution with pancreatitis, gallbladder disease, severe stomach problems, kidney disease and eye disease.

- Always have a medical assessment first.

- Tell all doctors you are using one, especially before surgery.

#### Frequently asked questions

**Who should not take semaglutide or tirzepatide?**

People with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid cancer or MEN2, those with a serious allergy to the medicine, and pregnant or breastfeeding women. Others need special caution.

**Can I take a GLP-1 medicine if I had pancreatitis?**

Usually this needs specialist advice, and many doctors avoid it. The risk depends on the cause and timing of the earlier episode.

**Is it safe to take GLP-1 medicines when planning pregnancy?**

They are not recommended in pregnancy. Plan with your doctor to stop them in advance and switch to safer options for blood sugar and weight control.

**Can people with type 1 diabetes use GLP-1 medicines?**

They are not standard for type 1 diabetes and do not replace insulin. Any use should be under specialist supervision.

**Do GLP-1 medicines affect surgery or anaesthesia?**

They slow stomach emptying, which may raise the risk of food remaining in the stomach during anaesthesia. Tell your surgeon and anaesthetist so they can advise you on pausing.

**Can older adults use GLP-1 medicines?**

Many can, but frail or underweight older adults need care, since weight loss may include muscle. Doses and goals should be individualised.
