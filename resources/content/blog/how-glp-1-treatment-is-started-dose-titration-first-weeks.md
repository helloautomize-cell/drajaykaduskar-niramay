**Quick answer.** GLP-1 treatment starts with a medical assessment, followed by a low starting dose that is increased in steps every few weeks. This slow increase, called titration, helps the body adjust and reduces nausea and other side effects. Most people reach the working dose within about 2 to 4 months. Regular follow-up lets the doctor adjust the dose, check side effects and support diet and activity.

Starting a new injection can feel daunting. Knowing what the first weeks look like helps you feel prepared. This guide explains how treatment usually begins, why the dose goes up slowly and what to expect.

#### Step 1: The Medical Assessment

Before the first dose, your doctor will:

- Review your medical history and family history

- Check for conditions where these medicines are unsafe

- Review all your current medicines

- Measure weight, BMI, waist and blood pressure

- Order blood tests, such as HbA1c, kidney and liver tests

- Check eyes if you have diabetes

- Discuss goals, expected results, side effects and costs

#### Step 2: Choosing the Medicine and Form

The medicine, brand, pen and form (injection or tablet) are chosen based on your condition, goals and availability. Your doctor will teach you or a family member how to use the pen.

#### Step 3: Starting Low

The first dose is a small starting dose. It is not meant to give the full effect. Its job is to let your gut and brain get used to the medicine.

#### Step 4: Slow Dose Increase (Titration)

Most schedules increase the dose every 4 weeks, depending on the medicine. For example, weekly injections often start at the lowest dose for four weeks, then step up. The exact schedule depends on the product. Your doctor will follow the approved plan for your medicine.

The dose may be:

- **Increased** if you tolerate it well and need more effect

- **Held** at the same level if side effects are present

- **Reduced** if side effects are troublesome

You do not need to reach the highest dose to benefit.

#### What Do the First Weeks Feel Like?

- Reduced appetite and feeling full sooner

- Mild nausea, burping or bloating

- Loose stools or constipation

- Fatigue in some people

- Slow weight change at first, with more visible loss after a few months

- Lower blood sugar readings in people with diabetes

#### How Can You Make the First Weeks Easier?

- Eat small, slow meals and stop when full.

- Avoid heavy, oily and very sweet foods.

- Drink enough water.

- Keep protein in every meal.

- Do not lie down right after meals.

- Inject on the same day each week.

- Keep a simple diary of dose, symptoms, weight and sugar readings.

#### What About Other Diabetes Medicines?

If you take insulin or a sulfonylurea (such as glimepiride), your doctor may reduce these doses to avoid low blood sugar as the GLP-1 medicine works. Never adjust them on your own.

#### What Follow-Up Should You Expect?

- A review within 2 to 4 weeks

- Dose reviews at each step

- Blood tests after a few months

- Support from a dietitian or exercise guide

- Regular weight, waist and blood sugar tracking

#### What If You Miss a Dose?

For weekly injections, instructions usually allow a missed dose to be taken within a few days of the due date. If it is much later, skip it and continue on schedule. Follow the instructions for your specific medicine, and ask your doctor if unsure.

#### When Should You See a Doctor Urgently?

Contact your doctor if you have severe or persistent stomach pain, repeated vomiting, unable to drink fluids, signs of dehydration, severe low sugar symptoms or allergic signs.

#### Key Takeaways

- Treatment starts with a careful assessment.

- The dose starts low and is increased slowly every few weeks.

- Mild stomach effects are common early on and usually settle.

- Diet habits and follow-up make the first weeks easier.

- Other diabetes medicines may need adjustment.

#### Frequently asked questions

**Why is the GLP-1 dose increased slowly?**

A slow increase gives the digestive system and brain time to adjust, reducing nausea, vomiting and other side effects. It improves tolerance and helps people stay on treatment.

**How long does it take to reach the full dose?**

Often 2 to 4 months or longer, depending on the medicine and your tolerance. Not everyone needs the highest dose, and your doctor decides the target for you.

**When will I start losing weight on a GLP-1?**

Many notice reduced appetite within the first weeks, with weight loss becoming clearer after the dose rises over a few months. Results vary.

**What should I eat when I start a GLP-1 medicine?**

Small, protein-rich meals with vegetables and whole grains are best. Avoid oily, fried and very sweet foods. Eat slowly and stop when full.

**What if I miss a weekly dose?**

Follow the instructions for your medicine. Many allow taking the missed dose within a few days. If it is much later, skip it and continue as scheduled. Ask your doctor if unsure.

**Will I need to adjust my other diabetes medicines?**

Possibly. Insulin and sulfonylureas may need to be lowered to prevent low blood sugar. Your doctor will guide changes. Do not adjust them yourself.
