**Quick answer.** A glucometer measures your blood sugar at one moment, using a drop of blood from a finger-prick. A CGM uses a small sensor on the arm or abdomen to measure sugar in the fluid under the skin every few minutes, showing trends and patterns. A CGM is more informative and convenient, especially for people on insulin, but costs more. A glucometer is still reliable, affordable and useful for many people.

For decades, a finger-prick glucometer was the only way to check blood sugar at home. Now continuous glucose monitors (CGMs) show sugar levels all day and night. Both have a place. Understanding the difference helps you choose well.

#### How Does a Glucometer Work?

You prick your finger, place a drop of blood on a test strip and the meter shows a number. It gives a precise reading for that moment. Most people test fasting, before meals or two hours after meals, as advised.

#### How Does a CGM Work?

A tiny sensor filament sits just under the skin, held by a patch. It measures glucose in the interstitial fluid every one to five minutes. A reader or phone displays the current level, the trend arrow and a graph. Sensors usually last 10 to 15 days and then need replacing.

#### What Are the Differences?

| Feature | Glucometer | CGM |
|---|---|---|
| Readings | Single points | Continuous, every few minutes |
| Finger-pricks | Several a day | Few or none |
| Shows trends | No | Yes, with arrows and graphs |
| Alarms for low or high | No | Yes, on many devices |
| Cost | Lower | Higher |
| Wear | None | Sensor patch for 10 to 15 days |
| Accuracy | Direct blood reading | Slight lag and small differences |
| Reports | Manual diary | Automatic reports for the doctor |

#### What Are the Benefits of a CGM?

- You see how food, exercise, stress and sleep affect your sugar.

- It can warn you about low blood sugar, which is especially valuable if you use insulin or sulfonylureas.

- It reveals night-time lows and early-morning highs.

- It reduces the number of finger-pricks.

- Reports help your doctor adjust treatment more precisely.

- It can motivate behaviour change.

#### What Are the Limitations?

- Higher cost, and in some cases limited availability

- A small lag compared with blood readings

- Occasional sensor errors, skin irritation or sensor falling off

- Readings should be checked with a finger-prick when symptoms do not match

- Data overload can cause anxiety in some people

#### Who Benefits Most from a CGM?

- People on multiple daily insulin injections

- People with frequent or unrecognised low blood sugar

- Those with unpredictable sugar levels

- Pregnant women with diabetes

- People wanting to understand food and activity effects, in consultation with the doctor

#### When Is a Glucometer Enough?

For many people with stable type 2 diabetes on tablets, a glucometer used at planned times is enough. A CGM can also be used for short periods, such as 10 to 14 days, to review patterns and adjust treatment.

#### Can You Use Both?

Yes. Many people with a CGM still keep a glucometer for checking symptoms, confirming unusual readings or when a sensor fails.

#### When Should You See a Doctor?

See your doctor if your readings are often above target, you have low sugar symptoms, or you want to know whether a CGM suits you.

#### Key Takeaways

- A glucometer gives a single reading. A CGM gives continuous readings and trends.

- CGM is especially helpful for people on insulin or with low sugar episodes.

- A glucometer is affordable and enough for many people with stable diabetes.

- Short-term CGM use can guide treatment.

- Your doctor can help you decide.

#### Frequently asked questions

**Is CGM better than a glucometer?**

A CGM gives more information, including trends and alarms, and is especially useful for people on insulin. A glucometer is still accurate, cheaper and enough for many people. The better option depends on your needs.

**Do I still need finger-pricks with a CGM?**

Often fewer. Many modern CGMs do not need routine finger-pricks, but you may check with a glucometer if symptoms do not match the reading or when advised.

**How long does a CGM sensor last?**

Most sensors last about 10 to 15 days, depending on the device. You then replace it with a new one.

**Is CGM accurate?**

Modern CGMs are generally accurate, but they measure fluid under the skin, so readings lag slightly behind blood. During rapid changes, differences can be larger.

**Can type 2 diabetics without insulin use CGM?**

Yes. It can help them understand food and activity effects, even in short-term use, though evidence of benefit is strongest in insulin users. Discuss with your doctor.

**Is CGM expensive in India?**

Cost varies by brand and duration. Ask your doctor for current options, including short-term use, which can be cheaper.
