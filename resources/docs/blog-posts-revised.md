**Summary box (show at the top):**
- Many people with type 2 diabetes have no symptoms for years, so testing matters even when you feel well.
- Normal sugar readings on treatment mean the treatment is working, not that it can be stopped.
- Insulin and modern diabetes medicines are tools, not signs of failure or harm.

Diabetes is one of the fastest-growing long-term conditions in India. In our clinic we regularly meet people whose diagnosis or treatment was delayed by something they had heard from a friend, a relative or a forwarded message. Knowing the facts helps you act early and avoid complications.

*[Video: "Sugar ki Baat: Diabetes and Obesity, Myth vs Fact" with Dr. Ajay Kaduskar]*

### Myth 1: "No one in my family has diabetes, so I cannot get it."
**Fact:** Family history raises the risk, but it is only one factor. Age, extra weight around the waist, inactivity, an unhealthy diet, stress, poor sleep and the "Asian Indian phenotype" (more abdominal and liver fat, less muscle) all contribute. Get your blood sugar checked from age 30, or earlier if you have risk factors.

### Myth 2: "I have no symptoms, so I don't need a test."
**Fact:** Many people with type 2 diabetes feel completely well for years. A simple blood test (fasting sugar or HbA1c) can find diabetes long before symptoms or complications appear.

### Myth 3: "I avoid sweets and I walk daily, so I don't need medicines."
**Fact:** Food and activity are the foundation of treatment. But type 2 diabetes involves both insulin resistance and a gradual fall in the body's own insulin, so many people also need medicines to reach their targets. Needing them is not a failure.

### Myth 4: "Diabetes means I am seriously ill."
**Fact:** Diabetes is a long-term condition that can be managed well. With good control of sugar, blood pressure and cholesterol, the risk of complications falls considerably.

### Myth 5: "My sugar is normal now, so I can stop my medicines."
**Fact:** Normal readings usually mean the treatment is working. Stopping on your own often lets sugar rise again. Any change should be made by your doctor after reviewing your results.

### Myth 6: "Diabetes medicines damage the kidneys."
**Fact:** Uncontrolled diabetes is a leading cause of kidney damage. Medicines are chosen with your kidney function in mind, several of them protect the kidneys and the heart, and your kidney tests are checked regularly so treatment can be adjusted.

### Myth 7: "Insulin is the last stage of diabetes."
**Fact:** Insulin is life-saving in type 1 diabetes and is often needed at some point in type 2 diabetes, for example at diagnosis with very high readings, during illness, surgery or pregnancy. Starting insulin when it is needed is a step towards better control.

### Myth 8: "I can stop my blood pressure and cholesterol medicines once sugar is controlled."
**Fact:** Diabetes care means looking after sugar, blood pressure and cholesterol together. Controlling all three lowers the risk of heart attack, stroke and kidney disease.

### Myth 9: "I should stop my medicines when I am fasting or travelling."
**Fact:** Treatment should be adjusted, not stopped. Talk to your doctor at least two weeks before a planned fast or a long journey, and check your sugar more often during it.

### Myth 10: "Diabetes will spoil my child's marriage or career."
**Fact:** People with well-controlled diabetes study, work in almost every profession, marry and have healthy families.

### Take-home messages
- Get tested regularly if you are at risk, even if you feel well.
- Eat a balanced diet, stay active and aim for a healthy waist size.
- Take medicines exactly as prescribed, and never stop them on your own.
- Check your sugar as advised and keep your follow-up visits.
- Control blood pressure and cholesterol as well as sugar.
- Trust your doctor over social media.

### Frequently asked questions
**At what age should I get my sugar tested?**
From age 30, or earlier if you have a family history, a large waist, high blood pressure, PCOS, or had diabetes in pregnancy.

**Which test is best for checking diabetes?**
Fasting blood sugar and HbA1c are the most common. HbA1c shows your average sugar over about three months and does not need fasting. Your doctor may also advise a glucose tolerance test.

**Can type 2 diabetes go away?**
Some people, especially early in the condition, can bring their sugar back to normal without medicines after significant weight loss. Doctors call this remission, not cure, because sugar can rise again, so regular checks continue.

### Parenting teens: still a challenge
Adolescence is a journey from childhood to adulthood, and parenting a teenager is a journey too: guiding a young person towards independence, passing on values, and helping them handle friendships and relationships. Every child is different, and even the same parents face new challenges with each child.

All parents want a happy, successful child, yet there is little agreement on how to get there. We often try to teach teenagers to obey without question. What they need more is to learn to disagree openly and respectfully, and to ask for guidance when they are unsure rather than acting on impulse.

### The three pillars: connection, monitoring and autonomy
**1. Connection.** A warm, steady and loving relationship is the background against which everything else happens. Teenagers who feel connected to their parents are more likely to be confident and cooperative with others.

**2. Monitoring.** Knowing where your teenager is, who their friends are and what they are doing matters. Within a warm relationship, this comes across as caring rather than intrusive.

**3. Psychological autonomy.** Encourage your teenager to think independently and express their own ideas. Listen to their feelings and show love without conditions. The opposite, psychological control, means changing the subject, personal criticism, withdrawing affection or making a child feel guilty for thinking differently. It pushes teenagers into their shell.

These three sound simple, but turning them into daily habits is hard, especially with a teenager whose favourite lines are "You just don't understand" and "Leave me alone". Separating from parents is a normal part of adolescence, and friends matter more each year. Even so, nothing replaces the parent and teen relationship. Spending relaxed time together, through hobbies, sport, a shared meal or a walk, keeps you connected and helps you stay aware of what is happening in their life.

### Discipline: shaping a bright future
Discipline is unpleasant for everyone, but a relationship built on warmth can withstand it. Teenagers rarely admit it, yet firm boundaries reassure them.
- **Set rules together, in advance.** Involve your teenager in setting family rules and agreeing the consequences before a rule is broken.
- **Stay calm.** When a rule is broken, enforce the agreed consequence without treating it as a personal attack. This avoids power struggles.
- **Separate discipline from control.** Help your child make sense of the world by offering explanations, but also listen respectfully to their view of their own experience. Trying to control how a teenager thinks rarely changes what they do, and often makes them withdraw.

### When stress becomes too much
Teenagers today face pressure from studies, exams, friends, social media and family expectations. Most feel stressed when a situation seems difficult or threatening and they do not have the tools to cope. Some become overloaded, which can show up as anxiety, withdrawal, anger, physical complaints, or unhealthy coping such as substance use.

How parents can help:
- Notice when stress is affecting sleep, appetite, mood, behaviour or studies.
- Listen carefully, without rushing to advise or judge.
- Encourage sport, hobbies and time with supportive friends.
- Learn simple stress-management techniques together.
- Seek help from an adolescent health professional early.

> **If your teenager talks about hopelessness, self-harm or suicide, take it seriously and get help the same day.** Call 112 in an emergency, or Tele-MANAS, the Government of India's free 24-hour mental health helpline, on **14416** or **1-800-891-4416**.

### "Smart Love"
The book *Smart Love* by Martha Heineman Pieper and William J. Pieper offers a compassionate approach to parenting, from newborns to teenagers. Its central idea is that a child's inner happiness is the foundation of good behaviour. In our own words, the ideas I find most useful for parents are:
- **A child is a child.** See the world through your child's eyes, and accept immaturity instead of fighting it.
- **Build optimism.** Help your child face obstacles honestly, without frightening them into pessimism.
- **Nurture inner happiness.** Lasting contentment comes from within, not from outside rewards.
- **Be the example.** Children learn from how we treat them. Constant criticism teaches them to expect unhappiness.
- **Happy children behave better.** Good behaviour grows from feeling secure and valued more than from constant correction.
- **Give time, not only "quality time".** Being present regularly matters.
- **Attention builds independence.** Loving attention does not spoil children; it gives them the security to become independent.
- **Find the middle ground.** You need not choose between being a pushover and being strict.
- **Trust yourself.** Your parenting instincts are usually sound; listen to both your head and your heart.

### In short
Parents who focus on guiding their teenager, rather than controlling them, tend to have more success and far less conflict. Give your teenager love, time, clear boundaries and the freedom to think for themselves, and you may find you enjoy watching them grow into an independent young adult.

*Reference: Pieper, M. H. and Pieper, W. J. Smart Love: The Compassionate Alternative to Discipline That Will Make You a Better Parent and Your Child a Better Person.*

**Summary box:**
- Self-esteem is how we feel about ourselves, and it shapes how we act.
- Teenagers with disabilities have the same hopes as other teenagers; barriers in their surroundings often hold them back more than the disability itself.
- Families, schools and health professionals can all help remove those barriers.

### What self-esteem looks like
Self-esteem is how we feel about ourselves, and our behaviour reflects it. It is one of the foundations of happiness and success.

A young person with healthy self-esteem is more likely to:
- act independently and take responsibility
- take pride in what they achieve
- cope with frustration
- try new tasks and challenges
- handle both positive and negative emotions
- help others

A young person with low self-esteem may:
- avoid trying new things
- feel unloved or unwanted
- blame others for their own difficulties
- seem indifferent, or pretend not to care
- become upset easily when things go wrong
- put down their own talents
- be easily influenced by others

### Adolescence and disability
Adolescence is a time of big physical, emotional and social change. For most young people, when their needs are met, it can be a fairly smooth path to adulthood: further study or work, new relationships and a clearer sense of who they are. When a physical, sensory, learning or developmental disability is part of the picture, building self-esteem needs extra attention.

Teenagers with disabilities want what all teenagers want: happiness, meaningful work, close relationships, independence, and to be believed in and accepted. What often stands in the way is not the disability itself, but prejudice, missing opportunities to learn skills, and practical or financial barriers.

These challenges show up in three areas of life:
- **External:** education, work and independent living
- **Interpersonal:** friendships and relationships
- **Personal:** self-image and the risk of social isolation

Teenagers with disabilities are more likely to have fewer friends and fewer chances to join in activities, and to spend leisure time alone. Over time this can lead to loneliness and a lower sense of being accepted.

### Where help begins: the family
Help starts at home, with parents, and the young person, accepting the disability as a different way of being able rather than a lack of ability. Seek help from a health professional whenever it is needed.

A good approach is family-centred and respectful. Teenagers should:
- lead decisions about the type and amount of support they receive, and
- be treated with respect at every step.

Rather than trying to "fix" young people so they fit society's expectations, we should work on removing the barriers around them: educating others, changing attitudes, improving physical access in schools and public places, and building links with community support such as transport, volunteers and care services.

### Tips for teenagers
- **Focus on what you can do.** Everyone has strengths and limitations. Acknowledge your disability, and build on your abilities.
- **Avoid unfair comparisons.** Compare like with like. A young athlete with a locomotor disability may not play in the Olympics, but can compete in the Paralympics.
- **Set realistic goals.** If reading takes you longer, allow the extra time and reward yourself for persevering.
- **Don't over-generalise.** Not being able to do one thing does not make you a failure. Do not tie your self-worth to a single skill or event.
- **Drop unfair "should" statements.** "I should finish this exam in 50 minutes like everyone else" may not be fair. Accommodations such as extra time exist so that everyone can show what they know.
- **Appreciate all of yourself.** List your strengths, including how you cope with your disability. Those coping skills are strengths too.

### Tips for parents: fair, firm and friendly
Discipline works best when it is fair, firm and friendly. When your teenager faces a problem:
1. **Clarify it together.** Ask questions that help them describe what they see, hear and feel, and what needs to change.
2. **Brainstorm options.** There is usually more than one solution; suggest some if they have none.
3. **Let them choose,** after thinking through the consequences. The best choice solves the problem and leaves them feeling good about themselves.
4. **Review the result together.** What worked, what did not, and why. This builds better decisions next time.

### Remember
Teenagers with disabilities are teenagers first. Like everyone, they want to be happy. They may not reach every goal, but it matters that they try and that they understand the obstacles they face. Families, schools and health professionals can help them build the confidence to live full lives.

- Childhood is the best time to build habits that carry a child through adolescence.

### Today's preparation shapes tomorrow
A child's nature is their own, but behaviour is learned. A well-behaved child is not a matter of luck. And if children learn behaviour, they can also learn to change it.

Children are often seen as a measure of their parents, and families feel judged by their children's behaviour and results. Think of how people choose apples, holding each one to the light and checking for blemishes. In the same way, we want our children to be perfect: happy, respectful, well behaved, motivated and independent. The goals are shared by almost every parent. Reaching them takes effort that starts in early childhood.

### Why parenting feels harder today
Many of us were brought up with strict discipline at school and at home. Today, with smaller families, most children are deeply loved and sometimes over-indulged at home, while still facing discipline at school. Parents ask: why don't the old methods work any more?

Part of the answer is that childhood itself has become harder:
- Children feel constant pressure from friends, school, media and sometimes parents.
- Working parents often come home tired, and patience runs short. Single parenting brings its own stresses.
- A generation ago, families in the same neighbourhood shared similar rules. Today every family has its own standards, and children see many versions of right and wrong.

Childhood often passes smoothly. Then adolescence arrives, and expectations change suddenly. In school, children get labelled as "high scorers" or "low scorers", "good" or "bad", sometimes simply because of the friends they keep. A teenager who feels labelled may become stubborn, and as parents and teachers tighten discipline, a cycle of misbehaviour can set in. This is why it helps to prepare, both ourselves and our children, well before adolescence begins.

### Love does not always light the way
A young child's mind is more flexible than a teenager's, so childhood is the time to shape behaviour. Every parent can make three promises:

1. **Courage.** Be open to new ideas. If what you are doing works, keep doing it. If it does not, have the courage to try something different.
2. **Patience.** Change takes time. We are used to instant results, from quick photos to instant meals, and expect behaviour to change overnight. A few days is not long enough to judge a new approach; some take weeks.
3. **Practice.** Every parent must practise, including me. My child does not care that I look after other children's health. At home, I am a mother, and I get tested just like every other parent. There is no magic wand.

### What helps change behaviour
- **Reward what you want to see, and calmly ignore minor misbehaviour.** Children repeat behaviour that gets rewarded and drop behaviour that gets no attention.
- **Be consistent.** Rewarding a behaviour one day and punishing it the next confuses children.
- **Know what is normal for each age.** This helps you tell ordinary phases from real problems.
- **Avoid physical punishment.** It is less effective, and harmful. Stories and examples that show good behaviour being valued teach lessons that last.
- **Build good habits early:** tidiness, personal hygiene, an organised study space. Habits are not inherited; children copy what they see every day, so sometimes parents need to change their own habits first.
- **Teach them to handle success and failure.** Studies, competition, achievement and disappointment are part of life. Learning to face them calmly brings lasting happiness.
- **Model healthy routines at home:** regular meals, physical activity, sensible screen time and sleep. Watch programmes together when you can, talk about what you see, and explain the difference between fantasy and real life.
- **Praise often, when it is deserved,** and correct mistakes privately, never in front of others.
- **Keep a simple behaviour diary** for children who find change hard. It helps you see gradual progress and stay patient.

### Keep going
The years when children need us most pass quickly. Most children come through them well, despite bumps along the way. Parents who persevere, with courage, patience and practice, give their children the best start to adolescence.

**Summary box:**
- Change a pad about every 4 to 6 hours, a tampon every 4 to 8 hours (never more than 8), and empty a menstrual cup at least every 12 hours.
- Wash the outer genital area with plain water, front to back. Never wash inside the vagina.
- See a doctor for very heavy or very painful periods, or periods that are very irregular.

### Periods are normal, and healthy
Menstruation is still surrounded by myths and restrictions. Many girls grow up without clear, scientific information about their periods, which can lead to discomfort and infections.

Some families still treat a girl as "unclean" during her period. This belief comes from a time when women reused cloth, had no running water and no modern soap. Together with a poor understanding of the body, it led to rules such as staying out of the kitchen, avoiding places of worship, or staying apart from the family. Times have changed. Periods are a sign of fertility and good health, and with the right care, a girl can stay clean, comfortable and active throughout.

### 1. Choose a product that suits you
- **Sanitary pads:** the most common choice; easy to use.
- **Tampons:** worn inside the vagina; useful for sport and swimming.
- **Menstrual cups:** reusable silicone cups worn inside; last several years with care.
- **Period underwear and reusable cloth pads:** washable options. Cloth pads must be washed well and dried fully in sunlight.

Choose what keeps you comfortable and dry. It is fine to try different products.

### 2. Change regularly
- **Pads:** about every 4 to 6 hours, more often on heavy days.
- **Tampons:** every 4 to 8 hours, and **never more than 8 hours.** Use the lowest absorbency you need. Leaving a tampon in too long increases the risk of a rare but serious infection called toxic shock syndrome.
- **Menstrual cups:** empty and rinse at least every 12 hours, more often on heavy days; sterilise in boiling water between periods.

Menstrual blood is sterile when it leaves the body, but it quickly picks up bacteria from the skin. Left in a warm, moist place, these can cause urinary infections, vaginal infections and rashes.

### 3. Wash the right way
- Wash the **outer genital area (the vulva)** with plain warm water each time you change, or at least twice a day.
- **Do not wash inside the vagina,** and do not use soap, douches or scented products there. The vagina cleans itself, and soap can disturb the healthy bacteria that protect against infection. Mild, unscented soap on the outer skin only is fine.
- Always wash or wipe **from front to back** (from the vagina towards the anus) so bacteria from the anus do not spread to the vagina or the urinary opening.
- If you cannot wash, wipe gently with clean tissue.

### 4. Bathe as usual
In the past, women were told not to bathe during periods, because bathing meant using a river or an open space. Today, a daily bath or shower is one of the best things you can do. It keeps you clean, eases cramps and backache, lifts your mood and reduces bloating. A warm shower on your back or tummy can relieve cramps.

### 5. Dispose of products safely
- Wrap used pads and tampons in paper or a disposal bag and put them in a dustbin, ideally one meant for sanitary waste.
- Never flush them down the toilet, and never leave them uncovered.
- Wash your hands before and after changing.

### 6. Prevent and treat pad rash
A rash can appear when a damp pad rubs against the thighs. Change often and keep the area dry. If a rash appears, keep the skin clean and dry and apply a soothing barrier cream after bathing. See a doctor if it does not improve in a few days.

### 7. Be prepared
Carry a small period kit in your school or college bag: spare pads or your chosen product in a clean pouch, tissues, hand sanitiser, a small towel, a disposal bag, a healthy snack and a bottle of water.

### When to see a doctor
- Periods have not started by age 15, or there are no signs of puberty by 13
- Very heavy bleeding (soaking a pad every hour or two, or passing large clots), or periods lasting more than 7 days
- Pain severe enough to miss school or that painkillers do not help
- Periods more than 3 months apart after the first two years, or very irregular cycles
- Tiredness, breathlessness or pale skin, which can be signs of anaemia
- Itching, unusual discharge, a bad smell or burning when passing urine
- **Fever, vomiting, a sunburn-like rash, dizziness or fainting while using a tampon: remove it and seek emergency care.**

Periods are a normal and healthy part of growing up. If you have questions or worries, Blooming Buds offers a private, respectful space to talk.
